Nobody knows how much a roll will shrink until it has been steamed

Knit fabric relaxes when it meets steam and heat. A body panel cut at 70 cm can finish at 69 cm. If the cutting room doesn't know by how much, garments come out the wrong size, and rolls with different shrinkage get mixed in one cut and twist apart in the finished garment.

Today the answer comes from a physical test. About one roll in ten gets a panel cut off, steamed, measured and logged in SAP. That is slow, it uses fabric, and most batches of the same fabric come back with almost the same number.

ShrinkX uses probability to say how much a batch will shrink, and how sure it is. You test only the batches it is unsure about.

Every test result wobbles in two different ways

Each dot on the right is one real batch test. Two things push a dot away from the fabric's true shrinkage.

Test noise (σ): two batches of the same colour rarely give identical numbers, even when the fabric is identical. Look at how far dots stray inside one lane.

Colour drift (τ): the dye route changes how fabric relaxes, so the true average differs slightly from colour to colour. Look at how far the lane averages sit from each other.

Think of it like this. Weigh yourself on a bathroom scale five times and the readings wiggle: that is test noise. Compare the average weight of five different people: that is real difference. ShrinkX keeps the two apart because only one of them goes away when you repeat a test.
σ tells the model how much to trust a single test. τ tells it how much one colour can differ from the rest.

First, a baseline for the whole fabric

One IM is one fabric specification, and all its colours share the same knit, yarn and spandex. So the model first forms a belief about the fabric as a whole.

With no data at all, it starts from a vague prior. In production this comes from tested IMs with a similar construction, spandex %, gauge and GSM. Here the default is "about −1% give or take 0.6". Each colour's average is then added as evidence.

Both sides are blended in proportion to how certain they are. The measure of certainty is precision. A vague guess has low precision, so a handful of results quickly outweighs it.

A tug of war. Two teams pull on a rope. The knot ends up closer to the team that pulls harder, and pulling power here is certainty. The prior starts strong only if you set its spread narrow.
More evidence and a vaguer prior mean the baseline follows the data. A confident prior resists it.
Try this
  • Drag "Colours included" from 8 down to 0 and watch the blue result slide back to the prior.
  • Set the prior spread to 0.10, a very confident prior, and see the data lose the tug of war.
  • Move the prior mean to −0.4 and note that eight colours still pull it back to about −1.0.

Now zoom in on one colour

A colour starts from the IM result, with extra uncertainty added because colours drift by τ. Then its own tests pull it toward what that colour actually did.

The prediction is a range, not a single number

For the next batch of this colour the model gives a best guess and a spread. The spread has three ingredients: what is still unknown about the colour, the noise of a single test, and the fact that the test only reads in 0.1% steps.

The 90% range means: if the model is right, nine out of ten future batches land inside it. You can check that claim yourself below by drawing imaginary batches and counting.

Stage 2, the batch correction. When a batch arrives with a different CPI or WPI from spec, the best guess is shifted by b × deviation. Your data has no CPI yet and GSM and width showed no effect, so b is 0 until real CPI/WPI arrive.

A probability model is honest about what it doesn't know. The range is the output, not a side note.
Try this
  • Press "Draw 500 batches". The bars should hug the curve, and about 90% should land inside the shaded range.
  • Set b to −0.05 and slide the CPI deviation to +4%. The whole curve slides left.
  • Go back a step and add tests. The curve gets narrower, but never narrower than test noise allows.

Turn the range into a decision

Two questions decide what happens to a batch. Could it be out of spec? That is the shaded area beyond your limits. Will it land in the shrinkage group we expect? That is the area inside the group band around the prediction.

Both are areas under the bell curve, found with Φ. If both look safe the batch is LOW risk and can skip the panel test. If one is borderline it is MEDIUM and one roll gets tested. Otherwise it is HIGH and gets the usual testing.

A weather forecast. "20% chance of rain" lets you decide on an umbrella. The model gives you the chance of a problem and you decide how much of that chance you will accept.
Spec limits and the group band are yours to set. They are example values here until you enter the buyer's real standard.
Try this

  • Read "How many more tests to reach LOW". Some limits can never be reached because test noise alone is too wide.
  • Switch to Width to see that its higher noise makes LOW harder to reach.

Watch it learn from your real SAP history

This replays the actual batches in the order they were inspected. For each one, the model sees only what happened before it, makes a prediction, and is then compared with the real result.

The first batches are HIGH because the model knows nothing yet. As results arrive, the ranges tighten and more batches turn LOW. That is the learning curve the real system would follow.

A good model has a hit rate near 90% for its 90% range, and it beats a plain average.
Try this
  • Press play and watch the first bands shrink as evidence builds.
  • Click any batch on the chart to jump to it and read what the model knew at that moment.
  • Change the spec limits in the Decision step, then come back to see the risk strip change.
The replay uses the original SAP values, not your edits from earlier steps. One +1.1% length reading is excluded as a likely sign error.
